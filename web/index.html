How two programs talk through a CPU cache_

I got two threads passing messages to each other without anything going through shared memory, the network or storage. The only thing connecting them is how slow the CPU's cache feels. In four short acts I'll show you how: you'll measure your own cache, watch my first attempt fall flat, send a message through it, and then try to shut it down.

The idea, in one picture

Here's the whole idea before any jargon. Picture two flatmates in sealed rooms. They can't see, hear or message each other, but they share one hot-water tank.

Every minute, the first flatmate either runs a long hot bath (that means 1) or leaves the taps off (that means 0). The second flatmate turns on their own tap and times how long it takes to run hot. Slow means the tank was drained: a 1. Quick means it wasn't: a 0. Minute by minute a message gets through, and nothing ever travelled down a pipe meant for messages.

Computers have a shared tank too: the CPU cache, a small, fast store of memory that programs running on the same chip share. This lesson is me rebuilding the flatmates' trick in your browser, one step at a time.

Act 1 of 4

Measure: find your cache with a stopwatch

You'll learn how a clock can reveal hardware that no program is ever told about. That's the core idea of a side channel.

Quick bit of background first. A modern CPU can do billions of operations a second, but grabbing a value from main memory (RAM) can leave it sitting around for hundreds of those ticks. So chips keep copies of recently used data close by, in a cache.

Think of a chef whose ingredients live in a warehouse a ten-minute drive away. However fast the chef is, every round trip stalls the kitchen. So they keep what they're using right now on the counter, what they'll need soon in the fridge, and only the bulk supply in the warehouse. A CPU does the same, with several levels of cache that get bigger and slower the further they are from the core.

When the data is already in a nearby cache, that's a hit: fast. When it isn't, that's a miss, and the CPU has to go further down to get it. A miss to main memory can take around 80 times longer than a hit.

The one idea to hold onto: how long a memory read takes tells you where the data was. No CPU has an API that says "your cache is 4 MB", but the timing gives it away. Information leaking through a physical property of the machine, rather than through an interface designed to provide it, is what side channel means.

The experiment

The plan is pretty simple. Take a chunk of memory, read through it over and over, and time the average read. Then repeat with a bigger chunk, and bigger again. While the chunk fits in a cache level, reads are fast. Once it's too big, they spill to the next level and the average jumps. Plot it and you get a staircase: each step marks the size of one cache level.

Browsers blunt their clocks on purpose. Why does this still work? 1 min

performance.now() is rounded to 100 µs in Chrome outside a cross-origin-isolated page, and around 1 ms in some other browsers. The panel beside the tool shows what this page actually gets.

That doesn't block the measurement, because it never times a single read. It times hundreds of thousands of them in a row (milliseconds of work) and divides, so the clock's rounding is a small error. What coarsening does cost is time: every sample has to run long enough to swamp the rounding. That barely matters here, but it's exactly what limits the message speed in Act 3.

How to read the chart and the table 1 min

Read the boundaries as brackets. The sweep doubles the window each step, so "~128 KB" means the level holds somewhere between 128 and 256 KB.

The "naive first-jump" detector shown beside the table is there as a warning: it reports the window after the jump and has no noise floor. A confident-looking number isn't proof the measurement worked.

Takeaway

The time a read takes reveals where the data lived. Time enough reads at growing sizes and you've mapped the cache, using nothing but a clock.

Fail: the obvious version shows nothing

You'll learn how hardware built purely for speed can hide the signal completely, and the trick that brings it back.

You might have noticed the tool in Act 1 reads memory in a weirdly roundabout way. That's on purpose. My first version did the obvious thing and walked straight through memory: read one 64-byte block, then the next, then the next, wrapping around at the end. And the staircase just… vanished. The curve came out flat, like the computer had no caches at all.

Why it's flat

Turns out two features built to make computers fast were hiding the slow reads from me:

  • The prefetcher. Walking memory in order is the most predictable pattern there is. The CPU spots it and fetches the next blocks before the program asks. In the kitchen: an assistant who has learned your routine and lines up the next ingredients before you reach for them.
  • Overlapping. Each read in a straight walk doesn't depend on the one before, so the CPU starts many of them at once and waits for them all together. The waiting overlaps, and mostly disappears.
Ran it live and your naive curve isn't perfectly flat? 2 min

Good catch, I hit this too. There were two separate things going on.

Wobbles at small sizes were the clock. Safari and Firefox only tick performance.now() about once a millisecond, and my old code timed each sample for just a couple of ticks. So every reading landed on a multiple of 0.25 ns, and a "2× jump" from 0.25 to 0.50 ns was literally one extra tick. The detector even reported a fake 64 KB cache from it. Now every sample is stretched to about 50 ticks, the table shows each reading's ± next to it, and the detector ignores any jump smaller than a few ticks.

A rise past ~16–32 MB is real. Once the data stops fitting in any cache on the chip, the walk has to stream it from main memory, and memory can only deliver so many bytes per second. Prefetching hides the waiting; it can't beat that speed limit. My native C version (nanosecond clock, no rounding) sees it too: flat at about 0.77 ns per line up to 16 MB, then roughly 0.9–1.4 ns from 32 to 128 MB. That's a gentle slope, nothing like the pointer chase climbing about 80× over the same sizes.

The fix: make every read wait for the last one

This is why the tool's default mode is a pointer chase. The memory is shuffled into one long random loop, and each slot holds the address of the next slot to visit. You can't know where to read next until the current read finishes, and there's no pattern to predict. So nothing overlaps, nothing is fetched early, and every read pays its full cost.

Peek at the code for programmers

src/cache/chase.js — the timed region

const t0 = performance.now();
for (let i = 0; i < steps; i++) p = buf[p];   // the address of the next read comes FROM the last read
const t1 = performance.now();
return ((t1 - t0) * 1e6) / steps;      // total time / number of hops = nanoseconds per access

That single line, p = buf[p], is the heart of it.

Takeaway

What beat my first attempt wasn't a security feature. It was performance hardware quietly erasing the signal. To see the cache, each read has to depend on the last and be impossible to predict.

Act 3 of 4

Talk: send a message through the cache

You'll learn how a timing difference becomes bits: a covert channel, where two parties talk through something never meant for talking.

Right, back to the flatmates. When you hit Transmit, this page starts two background threads (Web Workers): a sender and a receiver.

  • To send a 1, the sender churns through a large chunk of memory for a short time slot, flooding the shared cache. To send a 0, it rests.
  • The receiver keeps timing the same pointer chase from Act 1 over its own memory. When the sender is busy, the receiver's data gets pushed out of the cache and its reads slow down.

Slow means 1, fast means 0. The receiver never sees the sender's data; it only notices that its own reads got slower.

Spotting that the sender is busy is the easy part. Getting clean bits out of it took me way longer, mostly because both sides have to agree on when each slot starts. So each bit is sent as a pair of slots (busy-then-quiet for 1, quiet-then-busy for 0) and the receiver compares the two halves. The message is wrapped in a short frame: a warm-up pattern, its length, and a checksum that catches a flipped bit.

What do the numbers mean? readouts
  • Raw bitrate — 40 ms per chip, two chips per bit. Useful throughput is lower once the preamble, framing and (optional) error correction are counted.
  • Bit error rate — of the decoded message against what was sent. Short messages make this a coarse estimate.
  • Capacity — the Shannon limit, rate × (1 − H(p)), using the raw bit error rate across the whole frame. An upper bound, not something this codec achieves.
  • Checksum — one byte, sum mod 256. It catches any single flipped message bit, but it's weak against multiple errors.
  • Save trace — downloads the run as JSON: every latency sample, the threshold, the decoded and sent text, and the settings. It's the same format the native C version writes, so a live result can be kept and checked rather than just reported.
What this demo does and doesn't show limits
  • Replay is a simulation. It's seeded and calibrated to live measurements (~9 ns idle, ~90 ns busy), so it looks the same on every machine. The badge always says which one you're seeing.
  • Live adapts to your chip. The receiver first measures its own core's cache sizes with a quick sweep (the same pointer chase as the top of this page). Each cache edge suggests a probe size, a ~1 s pre-flight tries each one, and the best is used. That matters because the two ends only have a channel when they share a cache, and which cache that is depends on the chip and on where the OS puts the threads.
  • Live is real, and best-effort. If no probe size reaches 1.6× separation, the page switches to the simulation and says so. On my machine the adaptive version reached a live channel in 9 of 11 runs (the previous fixed-size version: 0 of 7). Of the live runs read back from their traces, 2 of 5 decoded cleanly and 3 had one or two flipped bits, each caught by the checksum. Runs started by hand did much better: 8 of 8 landed on the performance cores and decoded the full message intact at 9–11× separation (traces in data/browser/). Save trace keeps all of it, failures included, and records whether the machine was on battery or mains.
  • Timing is agreed out-of-band. The page tells both Workers when to start through an ordinary message, and both sides keep time against the wall clock from that instant. The payload never travels that way, but genuinely isolated parties would have to recover timing from the signal.
  • Manchester coding sends each bit as a transition (occupy→idle or idle→occupy). Here it keeps one calibrated threshold valid for the whole message and makes some errors detectable. Timing comes from the shared start instant, not from clock recovery.
  • Both threads share one process. The argument that such channels survive process, site or VM isolation comes from published research and how the hardware is built. This page doesn't demonstrate it.
Takeaway

A shared piece of hardware plus a clock is enough for two threads to talk, even though no data path connects them. That's a covert channel.

Defend: try to shut it down

You'll learn why defences against this kind of channel mostly make it slower and noisier, rather than closing it.

Your turn to be the defender. The receiver needs two things: a shared cache and a clock good enough to tell slow from fast. You can't easily take away the cache, so the natural move is to attack the clock, or drown the signal in noise.

Try it

These buttons work the Defence dial in the tool above and resend in Replay mode. Watch the bit error rate climb and the capacity fall.

The dial changes the simulation, not your hardware. It illustrates how noise eats into a channel; it isn't a measurement of a real defence.

What real defences do

In practice, coarsening the timer mostly doesn't raise the error rate. As Act 1 showed, the receiver just times longer runs of reads. What it costs is speed: longer samples mean fewer bits per second. Coarser timers, no high-resolution SharedArrayBuffer counter without cross-origin isolation, and throttled background threads all push the same way: slower and noisier.

A common intuition is that putting different sites in different processes (site isolation) should close a channel like this. It probably doesn't: it targets attacks that read another site's memory inside the same process, whereas a contention channel reads nobody's memory. It works because the cache is physically shared across cores and processes. This demo doesn't test that: both its threads live in one process. The cross-process and cross-VM evidence comes from published research and from the native version in the lab notes.

Takeaway

You can't un-share a cache in software. Defences make channels like this slower and noisier; closing them for real means removing the sharing in hardware (partitioned caches, separate physical machines).

Wrap-upWhat you just learned, and where to go next↓

What you just learned

  1. Measure. A read's timing reveals where its data was, so a clock can map a cache no program is told about: a side channel.
  2. Fail. Prefetching and overlapping, features with no security purpose, can hide that signal completely. Dependent, unpredictable reads bring it back.
  3. Talk. One thread making the cache busy or quiet, and another timing its own reads, is enough to pass a message: a covert channel.
  4. Defend. Coarser clocks and noise slow the channel down, but only removing the shared hardware closes it.

Why this matters now

In October 2026 OpenAI described a red-team exercise in which AI agents, placed in separate microVMs on the same host with no network link and no shared writable files, used a CPU cache covert channel to pass data: one agent evicted data from the cache while the other timed how long its reloads took (OpenAI, “Securing our frontier model training environment”; the agents were given attack objectives, and were often pointed at the channel). That's the same trick I'm showing here, just in a much easier setting: here, a page and its own worker threads in one process. The lesson carries over: cutting the network and separating processes doesn't stop timing on a physically shared cache.

What this is not. There is no exploit and no vulnerability to report. Both ends cooperate on purpose; nothing crosses a trust boundary. Cache and timing covert channels have been in the security literature for decades (Prime+Probe and relatives). This is a demonstration of a known idea, built carefully and measured honestly.
